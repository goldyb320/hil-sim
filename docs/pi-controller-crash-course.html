What the controller does

You pick a target speed (the setpoint). Every control cycle the controller:

  1. Measures the speed.
  2. Computes error = setpoint − speed.
  3. Computes P = Kp × error.
  4. Adds to a running total: integral += error × dt, then I = Ki × integral.
  5. Outputs P + I, clamped to ±12 V, and holds it until the next cycle.

The motor, in three numbers

It gains about 99 rad/s per volt at steady state, its speed responds with a time constant of about 0.1 s, and 12 V gets it to roughly 1,190 rad/s. Holding a given speed needs a specific steady voltage, about speed ÷ 99 (more under load). Holding 800 rad/s takes about 8 V.

P is a spring

It pushes in proportion to how far away you are right now and forgets the past instantly. At the target, P contributes nothing, so P alone can never supply the steady voltage the motor needs. With P only, the motor settles short of the setpoint, because it needs a leftover error to produce any voltage at all. That gap is steady-state error. Raising Kp shrinks it but never closes it.

I is a tank

The integral is a water tank. The error is a pipe: it fills the tank while the speed is below target and drains it while the speed is above. The I term's voltage is Ki × tank level. At steady state the error is zero, so P is zero and the tank alone supplies the steady voltage. That means the tank's final level is set by the motor, not by you:

final integral = (steady voltage needed) ÷ Ki

The rule that predicts most behavior

The tank level is just the area between the setpoint and the speed curve. So all the area stored below the setpoint during the rise, minus the area above it during any overshoot, must equal exactly what the motor needs at steady state.

If the rise stores more than needed, the excess has to be paid back as overshoot. The only way to drain the tank is to be above the setpoint.

Anything that makes the rise slow while the error is large (saturation, a sluggish motor, a high Ki) stores more area and causes more overshoot.

Integrator windup

With Kp = 0.05, Ki = 0.5 and no anti-windup, ask for 800 rad/s from standstill (press Windup above):

  • t = 0. Error is 800, so P alone asks for 40 V. The clamp outputs 12 V.
  • 0 to about 0.11 s. The motor climbs at full voltage. The error is large the whole time, so the tank fills, but none of it does anything because the output is already pinned at 12 V. The controller is storing demand the supply can't deliver.
  • About 0.11 s, speed reaches 800. P is now zero, but the I term alone asks for about 18 V. The motor only needs 8 V. The output stays at 12 V and the motor keeps accelerating past the target.
  • Afterwards. The tank only drains while the speed is above 800. The motor has to stay above the target until the I term falls from about 18 V to about 8 V. Result: a big overshoot and a slow, sagging return.

Anti-windup

Stop the tank from filling while the output is clamped. Storing demand the actuator can't deliver is pointless. Two common ways:

  • Conditional integration. While saturated, skip adding this cycle's error, unless that error would pull the output back out of saturation. The integral freezes at its current value. This is what the simulator uses.
  • Integral clamping. Limit the integral so the I term alone can never exceed the output limits.

Anti-windup must freeze the integral, not erase it. The integral holds real information: the voltage the motor needs. Zeroing it on saturation throws that away. The symptom is sudden vertical drops in the voltage command, often down to exactly Kp × error, followed by negative current as back-EMF briefly exceeds the applied voltage. A useful test: drive the controller into saturation and check that the integral is unchanged, not zero.

Real actuators all have limits: driver voltage and current limits, throttle travel, steering stops. Any PI loop that hits one can wind up. Deliberately driving a controller into its limits, safely and repeatably, is exactly what HIL testing is for.

Why PI and not PID

The D term reacts to how fast the error is changing. It anticipates and damps overshoot caused by momentum. That matters for position control, where a motor carries momentum past a target angle.

Speed control doesn't have that problem. From voltage to speed, the motor behaves like a simple first-order system, and it settles without oscillating on its own, so D has nothing to damp. Meanwhile D amplifies measurement noise (encoder speed is noisy and quantized) and produces a large spike whenever the setpoint jumps. That's why motor speed and current loops are almost always PI, and position loops use P, PD, or PID.

Starting gains

A classic starting point sets Ki / Kp = 1 / (mechanical time constant), about 10 for this motor, then chooses Kp for the response speed you want. For roughly a 20 ms response: Kp ≈ 0.05, Ki ≈ 0.5.

Experiments

  1. P only. The speed stops short of the dashed line. Raise Kp: the gap shrinks but never closes.
  2. Small step. Setpoint 100: clean, nothing touches 12 V.
  3. Windup. Watch the output sit at 12 V while the I term climbs. The I term peaks right as the speed crosses the setpoint, then drains only while the speed is above it.
  4. Anti-windup. The I term stays flat while clamped, then rises gently. Compare the overshoot readouts.
  5. Load step. At 0.4 s the speed dips and the I term climbs to a new, higher level. Then set Ki to 0 and watch the speed sag and stay down.
  6. High Kp. Look for chatter in the output. Drop the controller rate to 200 Hz and watch it get worse: the controller's timing matters, which is exactly what HIL exists to test.
